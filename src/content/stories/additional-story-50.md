---
title: "The Arabic Lesson That Taught Me About Humiliation and Loyalty"
slug: "additional-story-50"
---

Arabic was one of my strongest subjects. I was confident in my abilities, and I took pride in understanding the language and completing my assignments correctly.

One day, during an Arabic lesson, our teacher asked a student to go to the board and solve an exercise. The teacher checked the answer and declared it correct. I was surprised. The answer on the board did not match the one I had written, and I was convinced that my own solution was right. I leaned over to look at my Muslim friend's work and realized that he had made the same mistake as the student at the board.

I told him that his answer was wrong and explained how I believed the exercise should be solved. He listened to me and corrected his work.

Then the teacher announced that every student who had not solved the exercise in the same way as the student at the board would be checked. He warned that those whose answers differed would be struck with a wooden stick.

When the teacher checked my friend's notebook, he saw that the answer had been changed. My friend turned to me, distressed, and asked why I had made him change it. I told him not to worry. I would explain what had happened and take responsibility for my advice.

When my friend went forward, the teacher was preparing to punish him. I intervened and explained that I had been the one who told him to change his answer. I also told the teacher that I remained confident that the solution on the board was incorrect.

The teacher challenged me. He used an example with similar Arabic grammar — a phrase comparing a boy to a donkey, which we had to transform into another grammatical form. I cannot reproduce the precise exercise in English because the distinction depended on Arabic grammar.

Instead of becoming a constructive discussion, the exchange turned into a humiliating experience. The teacher used the example to make the class laugh at me. That moment stayed with me.

This memory is complicated by the respect I have always held for teachers. I believe educators deserve great respect, and it is difficult for me to speak about a teacher's conduct in negative terms. But respect does not erase memory.

What I took away from that day was a lasting awareness of the responsibility we carry toward our friends. I had spoken because I believed I was right, but when my advice placed someone else in a difficult position, I did not abandon him. Even when the outcome was painful, I tried to stand by him.

*Source note: Extended narrative from the Djerba interviewee's testimony for Echoes of Identity.*
