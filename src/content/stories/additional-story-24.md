<h2>Story 13. A Profession Learned Beside My Father</h2><p><em>Themes: craftsmanship · father and son · work · cultural inheritance</em></p><p>I have been working in this profession for as long as I can remember. In many ways, I did not choose this path; I grew up surrounded by it. My father was a craftsman, and from a very young age, I would accompany him to work in the medina. While other children might spend their days playing or staying at home, I was accustomed to being beside my father, watching him work, helping him with whatever I could, and observing the different stages of his craft. He never needed to sit me down and formally explain everything. I learned by watching, listening, and gradually becoming involved in the work myself. Little by little, what had initially been a way of spending time with my father became a profession that would eventually define much of my life.</p><p>This is how many traditional professions were passed down in Tunisia. A father would teach his son, who would eventually teach his own children. Knowledge was not always written down or formally documented; it was carried through experience, through the movement of one's hands, and through years of working alongside someone who had mastered the craft before you. You learned the materials, understood their qualities, recognized what worked and what did not, and developed a sense of precision that could only come with time. It was a way of preserving something without necessarily realizing that you were preserving it.</p><p><em>Source note: Adapted only by dividing the existing shoemaker testimony at distinct thematic transitions.</em></p><h2>Story 14. The Balgha and the Work That Once Sustained Us</h2><p><em>Themes: craftsmanship · father and son · work · cultural inheritance</em></p><p>In the past, traditional shoemaking was an important source of livelihood. People worked because they needed to provide for themselves and their families, and there was a practical purpose behind almost everything they produced. Traditional footwear, particularly the *balgha*, was part of everyday Tunisian life. People wore these shoes because they were familiar, useful, and suited to the way people lived. At the time, craftsmanship was understood differently. People recognized that making something by hand required effort, patience, and experience. Making Three or Four Pairs While Factories Make Hundreds</h2><p><em>Themes: craftsmanship · father and son · work · cultural inheritance</em></p><p>Today, however, the situation has changed considerably. The profession has become more difficult, particularly because of the competition from industrial production and the increasing availability of cheaper alternatives. There are people who can produce dozens, sometimes even hundreds, of shoes in the time that a traditional craftsman might need to make only a few. I may spend an entire day working carefully on three or four pairs, making sure that every detail is right, while someone else can manufacture a much greater quantity in the same amount of time. But there is a difference between producing something quickly and producing something with care. Quality requires time. It requires attention, experience, and a willingness to concentrate on even the smallest details.</p><p>This is one of the greatest challenges facing traditional artisans today. We are competing in a market where quantity and price have become increasingly important, while the time and skill invested in handmade products are not always appreciated. A customer may see two pairs of shoes that look similar and naturally choose the cheaper one without understanding the difference in the materials, the construction, or the work behind each product. Sometimes, people do not realize how much time goes into making something properly. They see the finished shoe, but they do not see the hours of work that preceded it.</p><p><em>Source note: Adapted only by dividing the existing shoemaker testimony at distinct thematic transitions.</em></p><h2>Story 16. Changing Materials Without Losing the Craft</h2><p><em>Themes: craftsmanship · father and son · work · cultural inheritance</em></p><p>The materials themselves have also changed over the years. Traditionally, we worked primarily with leather, using different types depending on the product and the purpose. We used goat leather, sheep leather, and other varieties that were available to us. These materials were familiar to craftsmen, and we understood how to work with each one. Today, however, things are different. New materials have entered the market, and customers have developed different preferences. We have had to experiment, introduce new fabrics, explore different combinations, and modify certain aspects of our products. We cannot simply continue working exactly as people did decades ago and expect the market to remain the same.</p><p>At the same time, adapting does not mean abandoning everything that came before us. There is a difference between allowing a profession to evolve and losing the qualities that make it distinctive. I believe we can introduce new designs, different colours, and improved materials while still maintaining the identity of traditional Tunisian footwear. We can develop our work without erasing its origins. For example, the *balgha* does not have to remain limited to one particular design or appearance. We can experiment with its colours, improve its construction, and make it attractive to people who might not traditionally wear it. The Old Olive-Wood Table in the Workshop</h2><p><em>Themes: craftsmanship · father and son · work · cultural inheritance</em></p><p>There is also an important question of quality. When you work with your hands, you develop an understanding of what makes a product durable, comfortable, and well made. You learn to recognize the difference between materials, and you understand that even small changes can affect the final result. A shoe is not simply something that covers the foot. It has to be constructed properly, it has to be comfortable, and it has to withstand everyday use. These are things that cannot always be achieved by rushing through production.</p><p>I have seen how the market has evolved, and I understand that people have different expectations today. Everyone wants something accessible, affordable, and readily available. I do not necessarily blame customers for that. People have their own circumstances, and everyone has to consider what they can afford. But I also believe that we should not forget the value of handmade products. There is a reason traditional crafts have survived for so many generations. Keeping a Traditional Profession Alive</h2><p><em>Themes: craftsmanship · father and son · work · cultural inheritance</em></p><p>Sometimes, when I look around my workshop, I am reminded of how much history can exist within a single place. There are objects here that have been around for generations. I have a table made from olive wood that is approximately 120 or 130 years old. Think about that for a moment: this table existed long before I began working, and it has remained here through so many years of change. It has witnessed different people, different generations, and different periods of life in the medina. Objects like these remind us that our work is connected to something much larger than ourselves.</p><p>I do not see my profession as simply a means of earning money. Of course, making a living is essential. This is my source of income, and like everyone else, I have responsibilities. But there is also something meaningful about continuing a profession that was taught to me by my father. When I work, I am not starting from nothing. I am continuing something that existed before me, something that was built through the effort of people whose names and stories may never be recorded anywhere.</p><p>I believe that traditional Tunisian craftsmanship deserves more recognition. We have so many products, techniques, and traditions that are part of our cultural identity, yet we do not always give them the attention they deserve. Sometimes, people admire traditional objects when they encounter them abroad, or when they see them presented as part of another country's heritage, but they overlook their importance at home. We should be proud of what we have inherited. Our traditions are not obstacles to progress; they are part of what gives our society its character.</p><p>I would like to see traditional craftsmanship develop rather than disappear. I want people to understand that these professions can still have a place in modern Tunisia. We can introduce new ideas, improve the way we work, and make our products more accessible without sacrificing the knowledge and techniques that have been passed down to us. There is no reason why a traditional Tunisian shoe cannot appeal to someone young, someone living abroad, or someone who has never even visited the medina. What matters is that we continue giving these traditions a place in people's lives.</p><p>Ultimately, I am doing what I learned from my father. I continue working with the materials, techniques, and knowledge that were introduced to me when I was a child. The world around us may change, customers may change, and the market may become more difficult, but I believe there is still value in making something with your own hands. Every pair of shoes represents time, effort, and a certain understanding of the craft. And as long as there are people willing to learn, work, and appreciate what we produce, there is still a future for this profession.</p><p>For me, preserving this craft does not mean refusing to move forward. It means remembering where we came from while finding ways to continue. My father taught me this profession, and I have carried it with me throughout my life. I hope that the knowledge does not end with my generation. Because when a traditional profession disappears, we do not simply lose a way of earning a living. We risk losing a part of our history, our identity, and the stories of the people who built it with their hands.</p><p><em>Source note: Adapted only by dividing the existing shoemaker testimony at distinct thematic transitions.</em></p>
