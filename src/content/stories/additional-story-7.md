<p><em>Source: Albert</em></p><p>Albert offers a concrete example of cooperation between Jewish and Muslim neighbours: assistance during Shabbat. He explains that observant Jews traditionally refrain from certain activities from Friday evening until Saturday evening. In the examples he gives, a Jewish resident might ask a Muslim neighbour to switch on a light at a particular time or to turn something off later.</p><p>The significance of the example lies in its ordinariness. It is not presented as a formal agreement or an exceptional public gesture. It is a practical arrangement between people who know one another and are accustomed to helping when a neighbour needs something. The request is understood within the rhythm of everyday life.</p><p>Albert says that Jews and Muslims could live in the same Hara in Djerba. When a Jewish person needed assistance, a Muslim neighbour could help. The Shabbat example makes that broader statement tangible: coexistence is visible in a small act carried out at a specific moment in the week.</p><p>The account also reveals how religious difference can be accommodated without requiring people to abandon their own practices. The Jewish neighbour observes Shabbat; the Muslim neighbour offers assistance. Each understands the other's routine well enough for the arrangement to work.</p><p>Albert's memory presents mutual support as something practical rather than abstract. It is expressed through attention, familiarity, and willingness to help. In this instance, a light being switched on or off becomes a small but meaningful example of how neighbours can make room for one another's religious lives.</p>
