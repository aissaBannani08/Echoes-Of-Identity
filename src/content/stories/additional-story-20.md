<p><em>Source: Albert</em></p><p>Albert gives several estimates of the Jewish population in Tunisia during the interview, including a figure of approximately three hundred people remaining across the country. He also recalls a much larger population in the past, around one hundred thousand. The figures in the transcript are estimates rather than a single verified count, but the contrast he is trying to describe is unmistakable: the community has undergone a profound demographic decline.</p><p>Yet Albert's account does not reduce community life to population statistics. He describes schools, synagogues, committees, businesses, family traditions, and relationships with neighbours. Some institutions remain active, while others have become harder to sustain. The synagogue still brings people together for prayer and conversation; committees help families in need; religious education continues in some settings.</p><p>He also remembers what has disappeared. Weddings that once brought families together are now less common because fewer Jewish families remain. Cafés that once held groups of Jewish friends may no longer contain a single Jewish customer. Commercial establishments and neighbourhood routines have changed alongside the population.</p><p>These details reveal why numbers alone cannot describe the experience of demographic decline. A smaller population affects the practical organization of religious services and institutions, but it also changes the social texture of everyday life.</p><p>Albert's testimony holds both realities together: a community can continue to preserve traditions and maintain institutions, while also experiencing losses that are difficult to reverse. Its remaining life deserves attention, but so does the scale of what has changed.</p>
